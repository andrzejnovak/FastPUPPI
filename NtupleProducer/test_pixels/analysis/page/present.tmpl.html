CMS Phase-2 · Level-1 trigger · simulation study

Can pixel-detector information recover the HH→4b events the hardware trigger loses?

Low-mass di-Higgs events carry most of the information on the Higgs self-coupling modifier κλ = λHHH/λHHHSM, and the planned hardware trigger drops many of them. We tested two ways to use pixel tracking: inside the hardware trigger decision, and in a 40 MHz scouting stream. Private simulation study.

Summary

In simulation, adding pixel hits to the hardware-trigger tracks raises the b-jet efficiency from 15% to 47% at 1% light-jet mistag, but the pixel readout chips offer no early readout path to deliver those hits in time. In the scouting analysis we tested, even ideal b-tags add only 2.5–9% to the expected HH→4b significance. The practical step is a b-tagged hardware seed built from the tracks already planned for the CMS hardware trigger.

For readers outside CMS

CMS's Phase-2 hardware trigger ("Level-1", L1) accepts 750 kHz of the 40 MHz crossings within 12.5 µs. Unlike ATLAS's Phase-2 L0 it has charged-particle tracks: the outer tracker's paired-sensor modules let FPGAs find tracks above 2 GeV for every crossing (ATLAS uses ITk tracks only after L0, in the Event Filter). The CMS pixel detector (inner tracker) is not part of that; it is read out only after an L1 accept. CMS terms are explained where they first appear and in tab 8.

Each figure has a "How this was made" box with the code and inputs; tab 8 gives the samples, background modelling, confidence of each result and open items.

The trigger gap

The tested pair of HT seeds (19 kHz, no b-tagging) keeps 60% of simulated SM HH→4b events and 36% of those below a generator-level mHH of 400 GeV.

  • Low-mass events have softer jets, so an HT threshold removes them first. These are the events most sensitive to the self-coupling.
  • At analysis level the loss is smaller, because the offline selection also prefers hard jets. Of the events a mock offline selection keeps (four jets, four medium or three medium plus one loose b-tags, both Higgs candidates in a mass window), the trigger keeps 60% below 400 GeV, and 63% of their κλ information over all masses.
  • Spending extra rate on lower HT thresholds recovers less than b-tagging does: about +15 kHz on lower HT cuts keeps 78% of that information, a b-tagged seed at a similar rate 92% (tab 6).

Where pixel data exist

In normal running, pixel hits leave the readout chips only for crossings the L1 trigger accepts. Using them in the L1 decision would need an early, partial pixel readout; the chips' documented architecture provides no such path.

Why the readout chip cannot do it

  • One selection latency per chip. Each group of pixels stores the time stamp of its hits. When the single programmed latency is reached, the hits are kept only if a trigger is present, otherwise they are erased. Setting it early for a request would erase every crossing without an early trigger before the normal L1 decision could accept it.
  • The two-level mode does not help. The RD53B design added a mode in which a first trigger marks crossings and a later "read trigger" command selects them; the command only flags a crossing, and readout follows a second programmed delay common to all crossings. It would need a first-level trigger for every crossing CMS might accept. Neither experiment's Phase-2 baseline uses two hardware trigger levels.
  • Bandwidth would matter only for a future chip. In an idealised model where requests are served before normal data, the requested chips (about 70 per crossing on average) deliver in about 1.9 µs median, 2.7 µs for 99%, including the chip's minimum 0.78 µs output delay. Queued behind normal readout, the slowest 1% wait 34 µs: normal 750 kHz readout already fills the busiest links to about 97% in this model. This is not an end-to-end latency budget.
For ATLAS readers

CMS's CROC and ATLAS's ITkPix chips come from the joint RD53 development; the production versions (RD53C) share one digital design, with experiment-specific features set by configuration. The two-level mode was added for an L0/L1 option ATLAS considered.

How this was made

Chip behaviour: RD53B manual v2.21 (June 2022), sec. 7.4 "Latency, Trigger and Readout block" and sec. 9 "Trigger" (one 9-bit latency, ≤ 511 crossings; two-level mode; minimum 31-crossing output delay, then queueing). For the production CMS chip (CROCv2 = RD53C-CMS), per-pixel trigger matching with one programmable 9-bit latency: TWEPP 2023 talk. The RD53C-CMS manual itself was not checked.

Readout timing: pixel clusters of re-simulated minimum-bias crossings (L1 HT > 140 GeV), mapped onto chips with a geometry learned from the simulation. Payload per chip calibrated to the CMSSW chip emulator. The emulator writes charge bits for every pixel of a 4×4 core; counting hit pixels only, the payload drops to 25–41% of the emulator value (range from the assumed per-core overhead). Output links per module (6/2/2/1 in barrel layers 1–4, from a CMS inner-tracker system talk). Queueing simulated with normal readout at 750 kHz; normal payload from 20 emulated crossings; no link framing; accepted crossings assumed as busy as average ones. Code: analysis/chip_readout.py, chip_readout_merge.py, readout_queue.py.

Pixels in the L1 decision

In simulation, extending L1 tracks into the pixel layers raises the L1 b-jet efficiency from 15% to 47% at 1% light-jet mistag, and a seed using it keeps nearly every offline-selectable low-mass event. It needs an early pixel readout (tab 3).

Why the baseline L1 b-tag is weak

  • The baseline CMS L1 b-tagger is a small neural network on up to 16 particle candidates per jet, including outer-tracker tracks from a five-parameter fit in which d0 is free.
  • The outer tracker starts at about 22 cm from the beam, so the d0 of those tracks scatters by about 545 µm around the reference; b-hadron decay products are displaced by a few hundred µm. Adding pixel hits at 3–16 cm brings it to 35 µm.

40 MHz scouting

In the scouting analysis we tested, analysing the crossings the trigger rejects with ideal b-tags raises the expected SM HH→4b significance by 2.5% with L1 jets, and by 5–9% with idealised generator-level jets. Multijet events with 3–4 genuine b-jets in the Higgs mass window dominate.

What 40 MHz scouting is

CMS's 40 MHz L1 scouting acquires and processes selected L1 trigger objects (jets, tracks, muons, …) for every crossing, independent of the L1 accept, for trigger-object-level analyses. ATLAS's trigger-level analyses work on events after its trigger selection; the difference is where selection happens. Pixel hits cannot enter L1 scouting (tab 3), so this tab asks a more generous question: if the scouting stream had ideal b-tagging, would the rejected crossings be worth analysing?

Expected discovery significance of SM HH→4b at 3000 fb−1 (Asimov, statistical only) from a binned fit of the reconstructed four-jet mass m4j. The range shows two background shape models (per-bin tagged events, or the untagged shape times the tagged fraction). Bars to scale on 0–1.875σ.

  • The rejected crossings in the Higgs mass window hold about 3 800 signal events against about 7×108 multijet events with 3 or 4 genuine b-jets among the four leading jets. With ideal tags those crossings alone give 0.24σ (0.35–0.47σ with generator-level jets).
  • Generator-level jets halve the Higgs mass window; the offline PUPPI jets we tried gave a wider window than L1 jets. The offline-jet test could not evaluate four tags: no simulated four-b background event passed.
  • With the baseline L1 tagger the gain is negligible: at 5% light mistag the significance moves from 1.070 to 1.070. Tighter working points leave too few simulated background events to evaluate.
  • Scope: a fixed analysis (four leading jets, mass pairing, m4j fit), so this is not a general bound on scouting. The four-b background rests on 16–19 simulated events, and b-jets from pileup collisions are unlabelled. The absolute significances shift by up to about 30% between background models; the scouting gain is small in every variant.
How this was made

Selection: four leading jets with pT > 20 GeV, HT > 150 GeV, two Higgs candidates paired by the smallest mass difference, an elliptical window √[((m1−124)/29)² + ((m2−114)/30)²] < 1.6 for L1 jets (centres and scales from the signal), fit in m4j with categories of 4 and 3 tagged jets. Signal: SM ggF HH→4b, 3000 fb−1.

Background: QCD multijet simulated in bins of the hard-scattering scale p̂T (30–470 GeV) with generator cross-sections and flavour, plus a dedicated sample filtered for ≥ 4 b quarks (about 3 000 events after the full simulation) for the genuine-4b part. Below L1 HT 300 GeV the QCD yields are scaled to minimum bias (tab 8). b-jets from pileup interactions are unlabelled, so the b-rich background is a lower bound.

"Triggered events": crossings the HT seeds accept, with offline-quality b-tag probabilities (b 84%, c 12%, light 1% per jet). Scouting covers only the crossings they reject; the two are combined in one likelihood.

Code: analysis/scout4b.py, scout_kin.py (BKG_MODE=factorised for the second background shape model); outputs derived/scout4b*.json, scout_kin*.json.

A b-tagged L1 seed

A b-tagged L1 seed using the baseline L1 tagger, at a measured +16 kHz, keeps 93% of offline-selectable HH→4b events below 400 GeV (HT seeds alone: 60%) and 92% of the κλ information of offline-selectable events at all masses (63%). Its inputs exist in the planned L1 design; firmware and full-menu integration remain to be shown.

  • 15 kHz is the rate CMS has shown for a standalone b-tag NN seed (a benchmark, not an allocation); it is 2% of the 750 kHz L1 budget (the CMS L1 TDR menu totals about 450 kHz including a 30% margin).
  • The same tagger can instead lower the rate: one HT + NN seed matches the HT seeds' efficiency at 13.7 kHz instead of 19.
  • On the analysis side, a softer offline selection (jets above 20 GeV, χ² pairing to the Higgs mass) up to triples the selected κλ information, but the multijet background grows by ×4.6–9.8; the information-weighted figure of merit I/√B changes by only ×0.99–1.15. Low-mass gains need better discrimination, not looser cuts.

Other channels

Among the tested channels, VBF H→bb gains most from better L1 flavour tagging; boosted Higgs and Z(νν)H are not limited by L1. Pixel-based versions face the same readout limit as HH→4b; the baseline NN tagger captures part of each gain.

L1-limited, tag helps

Methods & glossary

These are private simulation projections. Efficiencies, rates and sensitivities have different levels of validation; the table below gives each one's basis.

Samples

  • Signal: SM gluon-fusion HH→4b, official CMS Phase-2 simulation, 14 TeV, 200 pileup interactions per crossing; κλ dependence from generator mHH shapes at κλ = 0, 1, 2.45, 5.
  • Minimum bias (L1 rates): 1 999 360 PU 200 crossings; the 120 731 with L1 HT > 150 GeV re-simulated with pixel clusters.
  • QCD multijet: official samples in bins of the hard-scattering scale p̂T (30–470 GeV) with generator cross-sections (1.2 million events with offline jets, 140 000 with L1 objects), and a private sample filtered for ≥ 4 b quarks (Pythia 8, p̂T 30–300 GeV, about 3 000 events through the full simulation).
  • Emulation: CMSSW 17 L1 emulator (L1 tracks, PUPPI jets, NN b-tagger), plus our track extension into the pixel layers. Analysis in Python (numpy, awkward, scikit-learn).

Background modelling, and one pitfall

  • Rates come from minimum bias, which is the right mix of processes but has no flavour information for the pileup collisions.
  • Flavour comes from QCD samples. Below an L1 HT of 300 GeV these over-predict the minimum-bias yield of four-jet crossings by about 1.5×. Our interpretation: each simulated event is one hard scatter plus 200 pileup collisions that already contain hard scatters, so a crossing is counted once per hard scatter in it. Above 300 GeV the four-jet yields agree within 10% (in the Higgs mass window the high-HT bins have too few events to test). We scale QCD to minimum bias below 300 GeV.
  • b-jets from pileup collisions have no generator label anywhere: backgrounds that need true b-jets are lower bounds.

Glossary

L1 (Level-1)
CMS's hardware trigger: FPGA boards, 12.5 µs latency, 40 MHz in, 750 kHz out in Phase-2. Closest ATLAS Phase-2 analogue: L0 (1 MHz, 10 µs, no ITk tracking in the decision).
HLT
High-level trigger: the software farm after L1, storing about 7.5 kHz. ATLAS: Event Filter.
Seed
One L1 trigger condition (for example "HT above 450 GeV"); the menu is the list of seeds sharing the 750 kHz.
HT
Scalar sum of jet pT (here L1 jets above 30 GeV, |η| < 2.4).
L1 tracks
Tracks found in FPGAs for every crossing from the outer tracker's paired-sensor ("pT") modules, which keep only hit pairs consistent with pT > 2 GeV.
p̂T
Transverse momentum of the hard 2→2 scattering in the generator, used to bin QCD samples.
mHH, m4j
Generator-level Higgs-pair mass; reconstructed four-jet mass used in the fits.
PUPPI
Pileup-per-particle identification: weights particles by their likelihood to come from pileup; used offline and in the L1 correlator.
L1 NN b-tagger
A small neural network on the particle candidates of an L1 jet (including displaced L1 tracks) that outputs b, c, light and gluon scores.
Inner tracker / pixels
The CMS Phase-2 pixel detector (4 barrel layers at 3–16 cm, forward and endcap disks), read out by CROC chips only on L1 accept.
CROC / ITkPix / RD53
The CMS and ATLAS pixel readout chips from the joint RD53 development (production versions: RD53C).
40 MHz L1 scouting
A CMS system that acquires and processes L1 trigger objects for every crossing, independent of the L1 accept.
UParT
CMS's offline transformer-based flavour tagger (ATLAS analogue: GN2).
Offline-selectable
Events a mock offline HH→4b selection would keep: four offline jets above 30 GeV, four medium or three medium plus one loose b-tags (UParT; 1% and 10% light mistag), both Higgs candidates in the mass window.
Perfect b tag
Generator-labelled b-jets with no inefficiency or mistag; b-jets from pileup collisions carry no label.
