HH→4b L1 b-Tag Pilot

Is better b-tagging at Level-1 worth pursuing for HH→4b, for example by bringing pixel-detector information into the L1 jet tagger? This page first walks through what L1 sees today (Run 3), what the Phase-2 upgrade plans, and what adding pixel information would take. Then it presents the sensitivity study.

Short answer, now measured directly against minimum-bias rate: on top of the planned Phase-2 menu seeds, pixel information is worth ×1.06–1.11 the HL-LHC luminosity for SM HH→4b, ×1.07–1.15 for κλ = 0 and ×1.11–1.24 for κλ = 5. As a standalone seed it is worth ×1.12–1.25 for SM, but the menu already catches most of those events. The gain survives a realistic L1 path (L1 tracks extended into the pixel layers do as well as HLT pixel tracks). After an analysis-level selection (4b + 3b) the SM gain is ×1.05–1.11, at the stop line set beforehand: the standard chain loses most of the signal offline, not in the trigger. κλ = 5 keeps ×1.2–1.5. The case rests on the self-coupling (low mHH) and on other channels. Jump to the headline, the results or the plan.

In plain words

The idea, the three questions, and where we are

The idea. b-quark jets contain particles that fly a few millimetres before decaying, so some of their tracks do not point back exactly to the collision. The pixel detector, closest to the beam, measures this displacement far more precisely than the outer tracker that L1 uses today. The Level-1 trigger (L1), which decides in microseconds which collisions to keep, never looks at the pixels. If L1 could, it might recognise b-jets much better and keep more HH→4b events.

  1. Would better b-jet identification at L1 help HH→4b? L1 has a fixed rate budget. Rejecting more fake b-jets lets the energy threshold come down, and HH→4b events are fairly low-energy, so a lower threshold keeps more of them. Measured against the trigger seeds CMS already plans, and for events an analysis would actually select, it is worth only 5–10% of luminosity for the Standard Model (×1.05–1.11): most signal is lost offline, not in the trigger. It is worth 20–50% for a larger Higgs self-coupling (κλ = 5), whose events are softer. Answered
  2. Do pixel hits actually improve b-jet identification? Yes: the L1 tagger keeps about half of b-jets at a 1% fake rate instead of 15%. This holds not only with the pixel tracks the software trigger (HLT) reconstructs, but also when each L1 track is simply extended into the pixel layers by picking up the nearest pixel hits, which is something L1 could plausibly do. Answered
  3. Could L1 realistically get that information? Reading all pixels every collision is far too much data. The idea here is to ask for pixel hits only around the few jets L1 has already found, in about 1% of collisions. Turning those hits into useful information works in software (extending L1 tracks into the pixel layers keeps the full gain). Open: can the pixel electronics answer such a request in time, and does the fit fit into the firmware and latency budget? Open

Status in one line: pixels give a much better L1 b-tagger, also in a realistic L1-style emulation (2); for Standard-Model HH→4b the trigger gain after an analysis selection is small (1), so the case rests on the self-coupling and other channels; whether the electronics can deliver the hits in time is the open hardware question (3).

Terms used on this page
L1 / HLT
The two trigger stages. L1 is custom hardware (FPGAs) deciding within 12.5 µs for every collision; the HLT is software running on the events L1 keeps.
Correlator
The part of L1 that combines tracks and calorimeter deposits into particles, then jets. Layer-2 is where jets are built and tagged.
Tagger, tag score
A small neural network in L1 that gives each jet a probability of being a b-jet (and other types).
Mistag rate
How often a non-b jet is wrongly tagged as b. Lower mistag means fewer fake triggers, which is what buys a lower threshold.
Per-jet summary
Eight numbers that describe how displaced a jet's pixel tracks are (for example, how displaced the most displaced track is). It is one compact way to hand pixel information to the tagger; whether L1 hardware could compute it is part of question 3.
RoI readout
Region-of-interest readout: asking for the pixel hits only inside the cones of jets L1 has already found.
The headline

What pixel information at L1 would be worth for HH→4b

Measured directly, with the pixel tagger run on minimum-bias events: added on top of the planned menu seeds, a 2.5 kHz b-tagged HT seed catches 93.8% of fiducial SM HH→4b events with pixel information, against 88.9% with today's L1 NN, which is ×1.06–1.11 the HL-LHC luminosity for SM. For κλ = 5, where the menu alone catches only 48%, it is 75% against 68%: ×1.11–1.24.

The planned menu (QuadJet+HT and HT, 20 kHz together) already catches 79% of fiducial SM events, so most of what a better tagger finds as a standalone seed is already kept. The gain left is the 11% the menu and an NN seed still miss, of which pixels recover about 45%.

Without HLT pixel tracks: L1 tracks extended into the pixel layers

Everything above used HLT pixel tracks, which L1 will not have. The realistic version: each L1 track (Outer Tracker, pT > 2 GeV) is extrapolated into the 4 barrel layers and 6 disks, candidate clusters are taken in a window around the prediction from all ~58k pixel clusters of the PU 200 event, and the combination that best fits a helix with the L1 curvature gives a refit d0 and z0 (l1ext.py; no truth used). Against HLT pixel tracks, 87% of picked hits are the right ones, 75% of tracks get every hit right, and d0 agrees within 36 µm (HLT d0 errors are ~100 µm). The gain survives:

After an analysis-level selection: where the signal is lost

The question for an analysis is how many signal events are lost only because of the trigger. Offline PUPPI jets from the same events with UParT b-tagging (medium working point at 1% light mistag, measured on QCD; 84% on real b jets): at least 4 jets with pT > 30 GeV, |η| < 2.5; the four highest-b-score jets corrected for the measured b-jet response (a stand-in for b-jet energy regression; the response is 0.73 at 25 GeV and 0.96 above 150 GeV), paired into two Higgs candidates, and a 30 GeV mass window around (125, 120). 4b: all four medium-tagged; 3b: three medium and one loose. How to read it. The standard chain is far from efficient: only about 8% of SM HH→4b events survive this selection, but nearly all of the loss is offline (four jets in acceptance, four b-tags, correct pairing), not in the trigger. Of the selected events, the planned menu keeps 84%, adding today's NN seed 93%, and the pixel seed 97.5%. A better L1 tagger can only recover the selected events the trigger misses; for SM that is about 5% more events. It catches 8% more of all signal events at trigger level, but most of those are soft events that the offline selection does not keep.

About 830 selected SM events in the evaluation half; error on the pixel/NN ratio ±0.009. HLT pixel tracks give the same as the L1 extension (97.5%). For κλ = 5, whose selected events are softer, the planned menu keeps only 68% of resolved-4b events and the gain is ×1.24–1.55 (±6 points on the efficiency). Without the b-response correction the Higgs candidates peak near 105 GeV and the window keeps 31% instead of 42% of 4b events; the gain is similar (×1.03–1.07). Still approximate: no real regression (resolution unchanged), simple pairing, no HLT, which can only lower the gain.

How to read it

  • Why equivalent luminosity. The design spends no extra trigger rate; it changes what fits in the same rate. So the currency is how much more data the analysis would need to match it. If the background in the offline 4b region gains from the better trigger like the signal (it is mostly real b-jets), significance² scales with luminosity × trigger efficiency and the factor is the efficiency ratio (lower number). If the background did not gain, it would be the ratio squared (upper number).
  • Scale. ×1.12 on 3000 fb−1 is like adding about 360 fb−1, roughly a year of HL-LHC running, for this channel.
  • The pixel tagger uses the fake rate measured on minimum-bias jets (2.5% at 50% b-efficiency, 5% at 60%), placed on the idealised seed scan; this lands at 86% for SM at 14 kHz.

What is not in it yet

  • HLT and offline selection, beyond the generator fiducial proxy; an analysis-level background model.
  • Hardware losses: pixel information was taken from HLT pixel tracks, with no latency, truncation or readout effects.
Is it worth it? Not a dead end, but on HH→4b alone the realistic gain is modest for the hardware it needs.

The direct test, combined with the planned menu, gives +6–11% equivalent luminosity for SM HH→4b and +11–24% for κλ = 5, against a new regional pixel readout path inside the L1 latency that also has to preserve the normal readout. Two of the open risks are now checked. A realistic L1 path (L1 tracks extended into the pixel layers, no HLT tracking) keeps the full gain. An analysis-level selection (4b + 3b) brings the SM gain to ×1.05–1.11, at the stop line (×1.05): most signal is lost offline (acceptance, b-tagging, pairing), and the selected events are already 93% triggered with today's NN seed; κλ = 5 keeps ×1.2–1.5, because the planned menu misses a third of those even after selection. So for SM HH→4b alone this does not justify the hardware; the case has to come from the self-coupling measurement (low mHH) and (3) the same pixel information helping other triggers that need displacement: b-rich channels (HH→bbττ, ttH(bb)), charm, and displaced and long-lived-particle triggers. Stop criteria: if the κλ sensitivity gain (from a fit to the mHH shape, not just efficiency) and the other channels do not add significantly, this is a dead end for Phase-2.

Stage 1 · Run 3 today

What L1 sees in Run 3: calorimeters and muons only

The Run-3 L1 trigger decides within about 4 µs, from calorimeter and muon information, whether to read out an event, at up to about 110 kHz. The tracker is not part of the decision: its hits are buffered and read out only after an L1 accept, so the first b-tagging happens at the HLT.

Stage 2 · Phase-2 plan

Phase-2 L1 adds tracks, particle flow and a jet tagger

The Phase-2 L1 trigger runs at up to 750 kHz with 12.5 µs latency. The new ingredient is tracking: Outer Tracker modules pair two sensors and keep only "stubs" from tracks above about 2 GeV, which leave the detector at 40 MHz. The correlator combines tracks with calorimeter clusters into particle-flow and PUPPI candidates, builds jets, and runs a neural-network jet tagger.

The planned HH→4b-style seed in the CMSSW Phase-2 menu (step1_2024) is PuppiHT400 and QuadJet70_55_40_40 (jets |η|<2.4), alongside PuppiHT450 and SinglePuppiJet230. The tag scores already reach the Global Trigger in the jet word, but no seed in that menu cuts on them yet. The "today" baseline in this pilot is that system with the tag scores used, as in the tagger group's HH→4b validation (HT plus the sum of the 4 leading jets' b scores).

Where displacement information comes from today: the tagger's charged candidates carry the L1 track's dxy, but those tracks use Outer Tracker stubs only, whose innermost layer is far outside the pixel detector. Their impact-parameter resolution is much coarser than a pixel track's.

Stage 3 · if L1 used pixels

Adding pixel information: read out hits only around L1 jets

In the baseline the pixel front-ends store every hit for the 12.5 µs latency and send them out only after an L1 accept. Reading and tracking the whole pixel detector every crossing is out of reach: a PU 200 crossing has about 185,000 pixel clusters, 297 Tb/s at 40 MHz. That is why L1 tracking uses Outer Tracker stubs.

The proposal is a region-of-interest (RoI) readout: ask for the buffered pixel hits only inside the cones of likely jets, while they are still in the front-end buffers. The request does not need finished jets, only their directions. A seeded-cone jet is everything within ΔR = 0.4 of its seed, the highest-pT PUPPI candidate not yet clustered, so a cone around the seed contains exactly the jet; the seeds exist when correlator Layer-1 finishes, about 6 µs after the collision. A local fit in each cone gives impact parameters, condensed to 64 bits per jet, and the tagger runs again with them.

When can the request start?

The Phase-2 L1 design report fixes the timeline: the first L1 track data reach the trigger 5 µs after the collision (the full event then streams in over the time-multiplexing period), correlator Layer-1 must hand its particles to Layer-2 within 1 µs, the correlator must finish by 9.5 µs, the Global Trigger takes about 1 µs, and the pixel front-end buffers expire at 12.5 µs. Finished Layer-2 jets (about 9 µs) come too late to fetch hits and re-tag. Seeds may not: they exist once Layer-1 has produced the PUPPI candidates, roughly 6–7 µs. A cycle-level timeline (event streaming, seed selection, request distribution, return links, tagger deadline) is still needed.

The tagger needs impact parameters, not raw hits. Nothing in the Phase-2 plan produces pixel tracks within the L1 latency (the TDR states the pixel detector is not used for L1; pixel tracking runs at the HLT), so one of these has to fit in the ~2.5–3 µs window:

Information flow, step by step

  1. Seeds and pre-selection. At about 6 µs the hardest PUPPI candidates (or, slightly earlier, L1 track jets) are known; if the event passes a loose pre-selection (for example track-based HT), each seed's η, φ and z0 goes out as an RoI request.
  2. Regional readout. The pixel system returns the buffered hits inside each cone (ΔR < 0.4).
  3. Local tracking. Pixel tracks are fitted within each cone, constrained to the jet's vertex from L1 tracks, or a network learns the same information directly from the cone's hits (open question 3).
  4. Per-jet summary. 8 numbers × 8 bits: the three highest signed impact-parameter significances, the number of tracks and of tracks with significance above 3 and 5, the sum of significances, and the largest signed impact parameter.
  5. Tagger and Global Trigger. The tagger runs with the 64 extra bits per jet; its 8 scores travel in the existing 128-bit jet word, so the Global Trigger format does not change. The HH→4b seed becomes HT plus a b-tag requirement at lower HT.
  6. Storage. L1 objects, including the scores and the per-jet summary, are written with every accepted event and recorded every crossing by 40 MHz scouting, so the scheme can be studied offline in data. The full pixel readout after each L1 accept is unchanged.

These are orders of magnitude, not a readout estimate: the 806 comes from only 10 events (36 jets), a median times a mean jet count is not mean traffic, the pre-selection uses finished-jet HT rather than an early track-based HT, and real links carry encoded chip data with headers, module granularity and overlapping requests. HT > 140 GeV is the cut a 1%-mistag tagger allows at 14 kHz (see results).

Open hardware questions. (1) Can the pixel front-ends or back-end answer a regional request in the middle of the latency? The baseline chips send hits only after an L1 accept, and readout is destructive: hits read early for the trigger are gone for the normal readout after an L1 accept, so the scheme needs a separate copy of the hits or back-end buffering and re-merging. The chip's two-level mode does not solve this (see Plan). (2) Can request, readout and processing fit in the 2.5–3 µs between the seeds (about 6–7 µs) and the moment the tagger needs its inputs (about 8.5 µs)? No path from the pixel readout into the correlator exists today. (3) Fit or network? In the info-budget study, a small set network given the hits of pixel tracks without the fit added nothing over the NN alone, but it was trained on fewer than 2k jets. On the ~20× larger sample the same inputs do help (b-jet efficiency at 10% fakes 36% → 55%, 65% with a wider network), so a network can learn much of the track information from hits; how much data it needs, and whether it fits on an FPGA through hls4ml, are open.

Sensitivity study
Bottom line Worth a focused follow-up. The gain is modest for SM HH, large for BSM κλ and low mHH.

For SM events in a generator fiducial proxy (4 b jets with pT > 30 GeV, |η| < 2.4), the Phase-2 seed with the current L1 NN tagger captures 79%; an ideal tagger adds about 17 points (×1.2). For κλ = 5, whose events pile up near the HH threshold, it captures 59% and a better tagger brings that to 93% (×1.6; ×2.1 counting all events). Getting there needs a per-jet mistag of about 1–3% on the jets that set the rate, 5–10× better than today's L1 NN tagger. Measured on minimum-bias jets, pixel taggers reach about 2.5% fakes at 50% b-efficiency (5% at 60%), 7–8× better than today's NN; on the idealised map that is roughly 87–89% for SM, about half the ideal gain. The direct rate test confirms this for a standalone seed, but on top of the planned menu seeds the SM gain shrinks to ×1.06–1.11 (×1.11–1.24 for κλ = 5; see the headline). In short, the pilot shows substantial information in reconstructed pixel tracks and a measured but modest fixed-rate gain; it does not yet establish tagging from raw hits or a feasible readout loop.

The L1 math

Why b-tagging matters at fixed rate

The seed follows the HH→4b seed in the L1 tagger group's validation code (TrainTagger, bbbb.py): HT from L1 jets plus a b-tag requirement on the 4 leading jets. For each tagger, the HT cut and tag requirement are chosen together to give the highest HH→4b efficiency while the minimum-bias rate stays at the budget. A tagger with fewer mistags passes fewer minimum-bias events, so the same rate allows a lower HT cut.

HH→4b is soft. Of SM events with 4 b jets in acceptance, an HT cut alone keeps 99% above 150 GeV, 91% above 250 GeV and 69% above 370 GeV. Every step down in HT from where today's seed sits is worth a lot.

Result 3 · what it takes

Mistag rate decides it, at any rate budget

An idealised tagger tags true b jets with probability εb and everything else with εmis. At εb 0.5 and εmis 10% it gives 79%, close to the real L1 NN (80%). But on the hard minimum-bias jets the NN actually mistags about 21% at εb = 0.5, so the NN does better than a flat tagger at its own operating point (its seed sums continuous scores). Read the map as a sensitivity illustration, not a calibrated prediction; it also counts b jets from pileup as mistags at every point. Lowering the mistag from 10% to 1% matters far more than raising εb, because with 4 real b jets one tag is usually enough.

Where pixels stand

Can a network recover the track information from hits?

Trained to reproduce the HLT track fit (labels available for every jet), two cases. Hits grouped by track (4 hits in, no fit parameters): the network recovers each track's impact parameter (R² = 0.67) and its significance (R² = 0.75), finding 75% of displaced tracks (significance > 3) at 95% purity; it learns nothing with 10k training tracks and saturates around 100k. Hits pooled per jet without track grouping: a set network recovers track counts but not displacement (R² 0.17–0.51 for the displacement features), and tagging with its predicted summary falls to 18% / 43% at 1% / 10% fakes, against 51% / 77% with the true summary and 15% / 36% for the NN alone.

So the grouping step carries most of the value: something has to assemble hits into track candidates within each cone, either a simple track finder or a network that looks at hit combinations (for example a graph network). A plain set network on loose hits is not enough. All hits here still come from vertex-compatible HLT pixel tracks; all clusters in the cone would be harder.

Read with care

Caveats

  • Pixel tracks are not in Phase-2 L1. L1 tracking uses the Outer Tracker only. Everything pixel-related here is an "if L1 had pixel tracks" scenario; feasibility at 40 MHz is a hardware question.
  • Why 14 kHz. It is the default rate in TrainTagger's HH→4b validation, not a menu allocation. Results are shown from 5 to 30 kHz; tighter budgets favour better tagging even more.
  • The idealised tagger has no pT dependence and treats jets independently. It reproduces the real NN at the NN's own working point, which is encouraging but not proof.
  • Perfect tag. A gen-matched "perfect" tag reaches ~100%, but it ignores b jets from pileup (gen jets come from the hard scatter only), so it is not quoted as the ceiling; the εmis ≈ 1% points are.
  • κλ reweighting is in mHH only (cosθ* and pT,HH changes ignored). Our 14 TeV SM mHH shape matches the 13.6 TeV POWHEG κλ=1 shape closely (figure below). Weights near threshold reach ~90–130 for κλ = 2.45 and 5, leaving 6.9k and 2.4k effective events.
  • Seed not re-optimised per κλ, and only HT + tag was scanned; jet-pT thresholds and ORs with other menu seeds were not.
  • Statistics and optimisation bias. 655k minimum-bias events, about 295 passing at 14 kHz: ~6% counting uncertainty for a fixed cut. The cuts are optimised on the same events they are evaluated on, so the true uncertainty is larger; realised rates are 13.3, 13.7 and 12.9 kHz for HT only, NN and the 1%-mistag tagger (below the 14 kHz cap, not equal to it). To do: freeze cuts on one half and evaluate on the other, or bootstrap the optimisation.
  • Denominator. "4b in acceptance" is a generator fiducial proxy, not an offline selection; no reconstruction, offline b-tagging or HLT is applied.
  • κλ numbers are provisional. Generator weights in the Run-3 POWHEG samples were not checked, the effective sample sizes quoted are before acceptance, and no uncertainty bands are shown yet.
  • Detector realism. A momentum-space cone is not a detector address: magnetic bending, the vertex z uncertainty and module boundaries matter, as do barrel/endcap transitions (TFPX, TEPX), fake, shared or merged hits, inefficiency and a wrong L1 vertex. None of this is modelled yet.
Plan

What we can check now, and what needs insiders

Two decision gates, in parallel. Physics: HH→4b efficiency versus measured minimum-bias rate with an actual pixel tagger, on held-out events, including a realistic early pre-selection and request cap (the minimum-bias pixel production for this is running). Hardware: a concrete request and readout timeline from inner-tracker and L1 experts, including how to keep hits for the normal readout. Raw-hit networks, longer training and quantisation come after both.

Grouped by the three questions. "Now" means it can be done with the samples, code and public information we already have on subMIT. "Needs insiders" means it depends on non-public designs, firmware tools or detector expertise; for those, the public sources we found are listed first, so the questions to experts can be specific.

Read before asking. Region-of-interest pixel tracking at L1 has been studied in CMS before: pixel-based tracking around L1 electron and photon candidates (Moon and Savoy-Navarro, arXiv:1506.08877), and a 2018 proceedings noting that L1 b-tagging with pixel and outer-tracker information was being studied (arXiv:1812.00749). Earlier CMS discussion also described a "pull" architecture in which calorimeter and muon triggers request tracking in regions of interest. These are the natural people and papers to start from.
